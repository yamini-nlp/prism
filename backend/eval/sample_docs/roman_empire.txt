The Roman Empire began in 27 BCE when Octavian, the adopted heir of Julius Caesar, was granted the title Augustus by the Roman Senate, marking the end of the Roman Republic. Augustus is considered the first Roman emperor, and his reign initiated a period of relative peace and stability known as the Pax Romana, which lasted for roughly two centuries.

At its height, under the emperor Trajan in 117 CE, the Roman Empire stretched from Britain in the northwest to Mesopotamia in the east, and from the Rhine and Danube rivers in the north to North Africa in the south. The empire was organized into provinces, each governed by an official appointed by the emperor or the Senate, and connected by an extensive network of roads that facilitated trade, communication, and the movement of the Roman legions.

The Roman legions were the backbone of the empire's military power. Each legion typically consisted of around 5,000 heavily armed soldiers called legionaries, organized into smaller units called centuries and cohorts. Roman engineering supported this military strength, most visibly in the construction of aqueducts, which transported fresh water over long distances using gravity alone, and in the construction of durable paved roads.

In 284 CE, the emperor Diocletian split the administration of the empire into two halves, eastern and western, in an effort to make governance more manageable across such a vast territory. This division became permanent after the death of Theodosius I in 395 CE. The Western Roman Empire faced increasing pressure from Germanic tribes, economic troubles, and internal political instability, ultimately falling in 476 CE when the Germanic leader Odoacer deposed the last Western Roman emperor, Romulus Augustulus.

The Eastern Roman Empire, later known as the Byzantine Empire, continued for nearly a thousand more years, with its capital at Constantinople, until it fell to the Ottoman Turks in 1453 CE.
