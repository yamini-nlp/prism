Photosynthesis is the process by which green plants, algae, and some bacteria convert light energy into chemical energy stored in glucose. The overall reaction consumes carbon dioxide and water and releases oxygen as a byproduct. Photosynthesis takes place primarily in the chloroplasts of plant cells, organelles that contain the green pigment chlorophyll.

The process is divided into two main stages: the light-dependent reactions and the light-independent reactions, also called the Calvin cycle. The light-dependent reactions occur in the thylakoid membranes of the chloroplast. Here, chlorophyll absorbs photons of light, exciting electrons that pass through an electron transport chain. This process splits water molecules in a reaction called photolysis, releasing oxygen gas as a waste product. The energy captured during these reactions is stored in two molecules: ATP and NADPH.

The Calvin cycle takes place in the stroma, the fluid-filled space surrounding the thylakoids. During the Calvin cycle, ATP and NADPH produced in the light-dependent reactions are used to convert carbon dioxide into glucose through a series of enzyme-catalyzed steps. The key enzyme in this process is called RuBisCO, which fixes carbon dioxide onto a five-carbon sugar called RuBP.

Most plants use a pathway called C3 photosynthesis, named for the three-carbon compound produced during carbon fixation. However, some plants adapted to hot, dry climates use a modified pathway called C4 photosynthesis, which minimizes water loss and reduces a wasteful process called photorespiration. A third group of plants, including cacti and succulents, use Crassulacean Acid Metabolism, or CAM photosynthesis, opening their stomata only at night to conserve water.

Chlorophyll a is the primary pigment involved in photosynthesis, and it absorbs light most strongly in the blue and red wavelengths while reflecting green light, which is why most plants appear green. Accessory pigments such as chlorophyll b and carotenoids help capture additional wavelengths of light and protect the plant from damage caused by excess light energy.
