The Renaissance was a period of renewed interest in classical art, literature, and learning that began in Florence, Italy, in the 14th century and gradually spread across Europe. Florence's prominence as a center of Renaissance art was fueled in large part by the wealth and patronage of the Medici family, a powerful banking dynasty that funded artists, architects, and scholars for generations.

One of the defining technical achievements of Renaissance art was the development of linear perspective, a mathematical system for representing three-dimensional space on a flat surface. The architect Filippo Brunelleschi is credited with formally demonstrating linear perspective in Florence in the early 15th century, and the technique was later codified in writing by the artist and theorist Leon Battista Alberti.

Leonardo da Vinci, one of the most celebrated Renaissance artists, developed a painting technique called sfumato, which involves the subtle blending of colors and tones without harsh outlines, creating soft transitions between light and shadow. This technique is most famously visible in his painting the Mona Lisa, where it contributes to the subject's enigmatic expression.

Michelangelo Buonarroti, a contemporary of Leonardo, was celebrated both as a sculptor and painter. His sculpture of David, carved from a single block of marble, depicts the biblical hero at the moment before his battle with Goliath and remains one of the most recognized sculptures in the world. Michelangelo also painted the ceiling of the Sistine Chapel in Rome, a project commissioned by Pope Julius II that took approximately four years to complete.

Raphael, often grouped with Leonardo and Michelangelo as one of the three great masters of the High Renaissance, was known for the harmony and clarity of his compositions. His fresco The School of Athens, painted in the Vatican, depicts an idealized gathering of classical philosophers and demonstrates the era's fascination with reconciling classical philosophy with Renaissance humanism.
