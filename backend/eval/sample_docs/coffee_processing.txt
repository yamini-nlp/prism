Coffee begins its journey as a fruit called a coffee cherry, which grows on the coffee plant and typically turns bright red when ripe and ready for harvest. Inside each cherry are usually two seeds, which are processed and roasted to become the coffee beans used to brew coffee. Harvesting can be done by hand, selectively picking only ripe cherries, or by machine, which strips all cherries from the branch regardless of ripeness.

After harvesting, cherries must be processed to remove the outer fruit and expose the seeds inside. The washed process, also called the wet process, involves removing the skin and pulp of the cherry using water and machinery, then fermenting the beans in tanks for 12 to 48 hours to break down a sticky layer called mucilage. The beans are then washed again and dried, typically resulting in a clean, bright, and acidic cup of coffee.

The natural process, also called the dry process, is one of the oldest processing methods and involves drying the whole cherry in the sun for several weeks before the dried fruit is removed. Because the beans remain in contact with the fruit for longer, the natural process tends to produce coffee with heavier body and more pronounced fruity or fermented flavor notes.

A third method, the honey process, removes the skin of the cherry but leaves some or all of the mucilage on the bean during drying. The amount of mucilage left on the bean determines whether the result is classified as white, yellow, red, or black honey processed coffee, with darker classifications indicating more mucilage and longer drying times.

Once dried, green coffee beans are roasted at temperatures between 180 and 240 degrees Celsius. During roasting, a chemical reaction called the Maillard reaction occurs between amino acids and sugars in the bean, producing the hundreds of aromatic compounds responsible for coffee's characteristic flavor and aroma. A second reaction, called the first crack, occurs when trapped moisture in the bean turns to steam and causes an audible cracking sound, signaling the transition from light to medium roast.
